# Sidgwick, Russell and two ancient Stoics — 24 September 2026

This review adds seven positions and two people. See [coverage.json](coverage.json) for current corpus totals. This is a review of specific passages and their context, not a completed search of the authors’ collected works.

## Sidgwick

The [1897 second edition of *The Elements of Politics*](https://archive.org/details/elementspolitic01sidggoog) is the witness for all five records. Title/imprint and the relevant printed pages were visually inspected. None of these passages is silently dated to the first edition (1891) or to the Texas transcription’s fourth edition (1919).

| Record | Inspected passage | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Women’s suffrage | XX.4, pp.383–387 | Retains qualified support for otherwise eligible independent women and the contrary treatment of wives. |
| Animal protection | IX, pp.141–142 | Supports governmental prevention of unnecessary suffering; does not establish opposition to all animal use. |
| Religious non-coercion | XVIII.8, point 5, p.326 | Rejects compulsory imposition of settlers’ religion while encouraging missionary instruction. Qualified religious-liberty match. |
| Racial voting restrictions | XX.4, p.387 | Conditionally permits race-based exclusion on alleged inferiority grounds. Opposes the equal voting-rights benchmark. |
| Colonial dependency | XXVI.7, p.550; context XVIII.8, pp.323–327 | Retains imperial control under a racial/civilizational capacity test, partly to restrain settler oppression. Conditional opposition to self-determination. |

The excerpts across the entire work total **25 words**: 3 for suffrage, 4 for animals, 5 for religion, 8 for race and 5 for colonial dependency. These are short anchors, with full editorial context explaining the claim; the abbreviated suffrage quote preserves its original meaning and date. All five records share one source. Pre-benchmark opposition receives a negative foresight score at half weight.

Other leads remain open: *Methods of Ethics* discusses nonhuman moral standing, and *Elements* XVIII.8 pp.326–327 endorses eventual abolition with gradualist qualifications. No extra position is inferred from either lead here; one must distinguish moral standing from a specified reform, verify the selected edition and avoid duplicating an existing episode.

## Russell

The existing category is **Utilitarian & consequentialist ethics**, not exclusively classical hedonistic utilitarianism. Russell’s [*Elements of Ethics* (1910), III §§16–18](https://fair-use.org/bertrand-russell/the-elements-of-ethics/section-iii), supplies a direct consequentialist criterion. The relevant wording was also cross-checked in the [1910 *Philosophical Essays* witness](https://archive.org/details/philosophicaless00russ), p.25. The [IEP review](https://iep.utm.edu/russ-eth/), especially §§3e–g and 4, distinguishes this normative continuity from his changing metaethics and account of value; the [SEP review](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/russell-moral/) discusses the development in greater detail.

His consequentialist affiliation is therefore **core**, alongside core secular humanism. This changes default category filtering, counts and affiliation weights where relevant. It adds no new Russell position, and his existing extinction record remains provisional and excluded from historical rankings.

## Ancient Stoics

**Seneca.** [*Of Clemency* I.12](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/64576/64576-h/64576-h.htm), Aubrey Stewart’s 1889 translation, expressly permits executions for public benefit. I.14 demands prior reform attempts and I.17–18 condemn cruelty; those qualifications remain attached. The [scholarly chronology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/) places the treatise in 55–56 CE. This is a work/circulation proxy, not a known first-edition date. It supplies one plotted opposition record.

[Letter 47 §§17–19](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_47), in Gummere’s translation, urges humane treatment within slavery. The inspected collection’s [title/imprint](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius) presents a 1925 reprint; the ancient work-period proxy is 62–65 CE. This record is **unscored**: neither abolition nor opposition to abolition follows from the humane-treatment passage alone. Seneca’s approximate birth endpoint follows the linked biography’s c.1 BCE, internally astronomical zero, with the approximation disclosed.

**Epictetus.** [*Discourses* I.13](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Discourses_of_Epictetus;_with_the_Encheiridion_and_Fragments/Book_1/Chapter_13), George Long’s 1877 translation, rebukes a master by invoking common divine kinship. This is **reported teaching through Arrian**, as the [prologue](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Discourses_of_Epictetus;_with_the_Encheiridion_and_Fragments/Prologue) explains. It remains **unscored**, without invented emancipation or racial-equality claims. His [biography](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/) dates his birth only to the 50s and these teachings approximately to 108 CE. The stored teaching envelope spans the later career, c.89–135; the textual/public proxy spans Arrian’s second-century compilation, 101–200. Neither is a dated lecture, a dated first edition or a surviving manuscript date. A sourced later-career floruit replaces exact life endpoints.

Musonius Rufus was already included. Marcus Aurelius and the early Stoic fragment collections remain possible further research targets; no additional figures or scores are inferred merely from cosmopolitan language. In particular, moral brotherhood, humane treatment and inner freedom must not be turned into legal abolition credit.
