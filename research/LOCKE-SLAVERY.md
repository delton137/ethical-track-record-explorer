# Locke: ordinary chattel slavery versus penal enslavement

Reviewed 22 September 2026. The former `locke-slavery` point is removed: permission to enslave guilty aggressors is not adequate evidence of a position on ordinary hereditary colonial slavery.

## Verified personal writing

In *Second Treatise*, chapter XVI, §189, Locke denies a conqueror’s right to enslave the children of enslaved fathers. Section 182 denies that children inherit their fathers’ guilt; §190 asserts a birthright of personal freedom. [Primary text, Project Gutenberg no. 7370](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7370/pg7370-images.html).

This supplies evidence against inherited enslavement within his conquest argument. It does not expressly demand emancipation of enslaved people in the Atlantic colonies. Sections 23–24 and 172 still allow a restricted category of enslavement. The new record `locke-hereditary-slavery` therefore retains the narrower finding as qualified, unmatched evidence, with no plotted dot or leaderboard weight. Publication is late 1689 / title-page 1690; the passage’s composition date remains unresolved.

## Colonial counterevidence and attribution

The *Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina* explicitly protects racial slavery: article 110 grants masters absolute authority, and article 107 says religious membership does not change enslavement. [Primary text, Avalon transcription of Thorpe (1909)](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/17th_century/nc05.asp). Its heading dates the document March 1669; the [Cambridge editorial account](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/locke-political-essays/fundamental-constitutions-of-carolina-1669/5C787C7DD18D4AFE687EDCFB041F0BB4) distinguishes the July 1669 manuscript from the March 1670 printed version. Do not conflate versions or adopt the old transcription’s authorship note without qualification.

Locke’s involvement in preparation is documented, but treating each provision as his own ethical assertion is disputed. [James Farr (2020)](https://doi.org/10.5206/ls.2020.10310) distinguishes his substantial secretarial involvement from less certain authorship. [Holly Brewer (2024)](https://doi.org/10.5206/ls.2024.17536) challenges claims assigning proslavery provisions to Locke through manuscript analysis. [David Armitage (2004)](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/armitage/files/armitage-locke.pdf) is an important earlier contribution to that attribution debate.

## Result and limits

The checked evidence does not justify a simple personal pro-chattel-slavery or abolitionist dot. There is a verified rejection of inherited enslavement, alongside a proslavery colonial document with disputed personal attribution. Institutional involvement and investments are conduct and cannot substitute for a primary statement in this written-ideas corpus. This targeted review covers the cited passages and editorial/scholarly attribution discussions; it is not an exhaustive manuscript search or a resolution of that debate.
